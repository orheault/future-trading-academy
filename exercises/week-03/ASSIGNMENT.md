## Part A — First guided exercise

All figures below are fictional, not an upcoming release or market forecast.

Core CPI, month over month, on a consistent seasonal-adjustment basis:

- Previous: +0.3%.
- Consensus available before release: +0.2%.
- Actual: +0.4%.
- MES rises during the first five minutes after publication.

Answer in your own words:

1. What is the surprise relative to consensus? Show the subtraction and the unit.
2. How is that different from the change relative to the previous reading?
3. Classify these statements: (a) the released figure is +0.4%; (b) the surprise could push expected rates higher; (c) MES must fall. Which is a fact, a conditional hypothesis or an unjustified assertion?
4. Does the five-minute rally prove that the release was favorable for equities? Explain what is observed and what remains unknown.

## Part B — Transfer exercises

These are fictional examples, not market data.

1. Crude inventory change: expected −2.0 million barrels; actual −0.5 million barrels. Compute actual minus expected. Explain why a draw can still disappoint expectations, and name two other report components to inspect.
2. Gold rises while a comparable-horizon real-yield measure also rises. Does this falsify the claim that gold must always move inversely to real yields? Give one alternative driver as a hypothesis, and identify evidence needed to investigate it.
3. Oil spot = $69/barrel, nearby futures = $70/barrel, deferred futures = $72/barrel, all at the same time and with comparable specifications. Compute spot-minus-nearby basis; classify the curve between the two futures. Explain why $72 is not a guaranteed future spot price.
4. You cannot find a dated pre-release survey for a historical event. Explain how you would record expectations without inventing a consensus.

## Part C — Five briefs using the existing session template

Copy [SESSION-PLAN.md](../../templates/SESSION-PLAN.md) into `research/week-03/` for each case. Suggested filenames: `01-MES.md`, `02-MES.md`, `03-MES.md`, `04-MGC.md`, `05-MCL.md`. Files become evidence only when you complete them.

Add this information to each copy:

- Historical event date, actual study date, instrument and exact contract, session and chart time zone.
- Pre-event information cutoff and the time you froze the brief.
- Official event source, release time and time zone; source links and publication timestamps for every numeric claim.
- Separate previous, expected and actual fields, with units and period. Leave actual blank until the event is revealed. Label missing data `unknown`.
- At least two conditional scenarios, each with measurable expected evidence, a fixed evaluation window and explicit invalidation. Include the possibility that neither scenario fits.
- An observation reference and checkpoints defined before revealing future candles. The reference lesson provides one example.
- A permitted action of `observe only`; maximum trades = 0. Enter `N/A — observation only` for trade-risk fields. Do not invent technical profile/value levels not yet learned; mark them `not assessed this week`.
- A separately dated post-session audit with actual data, initial versus later price response, which criteria were met, what contradicted the hypothesis and one process improvement.

Historical-source caution: use information available by the historical cutoff, including original rather than later-revised releases. If you accidentally see the outcome, preserve the case and label the contamination; choose a fresh case for assessment.

## Part D — End-of-week quiz

1. Distinguish a scheduled from an unscheduled catalyst with an example of each.
2. Why is consensus different from the previous reading and from everything priced into the market?
3. Explain two competing effects of strong employment data on equities.
4. What is a real yield, and why must horizons match when estimating it?
5. Why does an inventory draw not establish stronger end demand?
6. Define contango, backwardation and your basis sign convention.
7. What makes a scenario falsifiable? Give a measurable example.
8. Why is observing a price move after news insufficient to prove its cause?
9. What should you do if you discover you used future information in a historical brief?
